# Local-only copilots dodge FedRAMP, not consent

As of late November 2026, **no federal statute, OMB memo or published federal agency policy names AI meeting notetakers**. They are controlled by default instead: agencies may use only AI tools that hold an agency authority to operate (ATO), sit on an approved-software list or are covered by FedRAMP. Anything else is excluded without a named ban. The approved federal notetakers today are features built into platforms agencies already authorize, such as Zoom AI Companion in Zoom for Government and Microsoft 365 Copilot in GCC and GCC High. Named bans on third-party notetakers exist only at state and local level: Georgia, Missouri for sensitive meetings, Bellevue WA, and Indiana's records agency advising against them. Three other bodies of law apply to any tool, bot or not: NARA's August 2026 guidance treating shared or official AI meeting summaries as likely federal records; state all-party-consent wiretap laws, which commentators say reach live transcription even when no audio is kept; and biometric statutes such as Illinois BIPA, which turn speaker enrollment into the riskiest feature. For a bot-free, local-only copilot, the good news is structural. If no vendor cloud ever touches federal information, the plain text of FedRAMP's scope does not cover the product, so approval moves to each agency's own software and ATO review, and on the contractor side the work falls inside the company's existing NIST SP 800-171 boundary. The bad news is that none of this overrides the meeting host. A client that disallows AI notetakers needs to grant explicit permission, and an on-device tool does not make that question go away.

*Sourcing caveat: the underlying research could not open most primary sources in full (archives.gov, fedramp.gov, Microsoft and several state sites were blocked), so most findings rest on search-engine excerpts of the cited pages. Items marked **unverified** or **vendor source** below should be checked against the primary document before any legal, contractual or product commitment.*

## Federal policy runs on an allowlist, not a notetaker ban

The government-wide AI rules in force are **OMB M-25-21** (AI use and governance) and **M-25-22** (AI acquisition), both from April 2025, plus **M-26-04** (11 December 2025), which adds "unbiased AI principles" for purchased LLMs on top of M-25-22. Together they replaced the Biden-era M-24-10 and M-24-18 after EO 14110 was revoked on 20 January 2025 and EO 14179 ordered their revision ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/31/2025-02172/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence); [OMB M-25-21](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf); [IRS IRM 10.24.1](https://www.irs.gov/node/156046)). A contractor blog reports that as of 28 October 2026 no later memo had rescinded M-25-21 (unverified against OMB) ([Pyramid Systems](https://pyramidsystems.com/insights/blog/omb-m-25-21-federal-ai-governance-explained)). None of these memos mentions meeting transcription. M-25-21 requires each agency to write a generative-AI policy and update its use-case inventory every year. It reserves its heaviest minimum practices (pre-deployment testing, impact assessments, monitoring) for **"high-impact AI"** ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/omb-issues-revised-policies-on-ai-use-and-procurement-by-federal-agencies)). An internal meeting summarizer is unlikely to fall in that tier unless its summaries feed personnel, benefits or enforcement decisions. M-25-22 applies to contracts awarded or renewed from about October 2025 and requires a clear split of data and IP rights between government and vendor ([Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/54731/2025-04-11-omb-issues-new-guidance-federal-governments-use-ai-and)). Clauses barring vendors from training on government data are likely negotiated under those provisions, though the memo's exact wording could not be confirmed.

Agency practice shows the allowlist at work. The **VA** bars sensitive data from any AI tool without a VA ATO that covers it, and lists meeting-note summarization as a use of its in-house "VA GPT" ([VA guidance](https://department.va.gov/ai/guidance-for-generative-ai-use-at-va/); [VA inventory](https://department.va.gov/ai/ai-use-case-inventory/)). **GSA** lists "summarize meeting notes" as a chatbot use case inside its FedRAMP-authorized USAi environment ([GSA](https://www.gsa.gov/artificial-intelligence/resources/ai-strategies-and-compliance-plan)). The **Army CIO's 2 September 2025 memo** brings AI tools under records, privacy and FOIA rules and makes system owners capture AI-generated records ([ExecutiveGov](https://www.executivegov.com/articles/army-garciga-application-owners-ai-records-mgmt)). Searches for DoD-wide, DHS, State, DOJ, Treasury or HHS policies naming Otter or Fireflies found nothing public. A November 2026 Washington Technology op-ed describes the Pentagon's standard reaction to rogue AI as another memo restating that unapproved tools are prohibited ([Washington Technology](https://www.washingtontechnology.com/opinion/2026/11/pentagon-wont-stop-rogue-ai-another-memo/417129/)).

The tools that pass this filter belong to incumbent platforms. **Zoom AI Companion** received FedRAMP JAB Moderate authorization within Zoom for Government in September 2024 ([Zoom newsroom](https://news.zoom.com/zoom-for-government-platform-expands-with-zoom-ai-companion-receives-fedramp-jab-authorization/)). **Microsoft 365 Copilot** reached general availability in GCC High in December 2025, with web grounding off by default ([Microsoft](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/publicsectorblog/microsoft-365-copilot-is-now-available-in-gcc-high/4473310)). The meeting-specific pieces lag, though. Teams Copilot in meetings (RM569214) and Intelligent Recap for DoD (RM500242) were targeted for October 2026, with roadmap entries showing conflicting status, and Microsoft documentation in August 2026 still said Copilot in Teams calls was unavailable in GCC High and DoD ([RM569214](https://mc.merill.net/message/RM569214); [RM500242](https://mc.merill.net/message/RM500242)). Whether these features reached GA by late November is **unconfirmed**. Cisco's developer documentation lists meeting summaries as unsupported in Webex for Government, which conflicts with third-party claims ([Webex docs](https://developer-portal-t.wbx2.com/meeting/docs/api/v1/meeting-summaries)). The FedRAMP AI prioritization track certified ChatGPT Enterprise, Gemini for Government and Perplexity, but no notetakers ([FedRAMP AI](https://www.fedramp.gov/ai/)). The research found **no evidence that Otter, Fireflies or Read.ai hold FedRAMP authorization**, though the FedRAMP Marketplace itself was not checked. The gap left by Microsoft's lag in meeting features for GCC High and DoD is a real opening, along with in-person, phone and non-Teams meetings.

## Shared summaries are federal records, which complicates "save nothing"

**NARA memo AC 11.2026**, "Guidance on Applying the Federal Records Act to Artificial Intelligence Materials," is dated 21 August 2026, though one source says September. It holds that using AI does not "in and of itself" create federal records. It also says that **AI meeting summaries circulated to colleagues or used as the official record of a meeting are likely federal records**, while notes kept only for one person's recollection likely are not ([NARA memo PDF](https://www.archives.gov/files/records-mgmt/policy/nara-fra-ai-guidance.pdf); [GovExec](https://www.govexec.com/technology/2026/08/national-archives-says-agencies-ai-use-does-not-automatically-create-federal-records/415714/); [Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/08/national-archives-says-agencies-ai-use-does-not-automatically-create-federal-records/415711/)). Records may be destroyed only under a NARA-approved schedule, and agencies must build records requirements into their AI policies. Commentators disagree on how broad the memo is. Just Security and the Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator read it as making prompts, summaries and chat histories records only "in narrow circumstances" ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/157670/federal-employees-ai-prompts-records-narrow-circumstances/)). The excerpts did not show whether raw audio or verbatim transcripts are treated differently from summaries, or which General Records Schedule items apply.

Records status carries FOIA exposure with it. No court has yet applied FOIA to officials' AI conversations, but the standard *Tax Analysts* test (the agency created or obtained the material and controls it) is expected to govern, with the usual deliberative-process and privacy exemptions available ([ABA Communications Lawyer](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/communications_law/resources/magazine/2026-fall/ai-accessible-under-public-records-laws-foia/); [Reason Foundation](https://reason.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Are-the-Goverments-AI-Conversations-Accessible.pdf)). The Army memo goes further and requires AI content on official platforms to be kept and disclosed for FOIA ([ExecutiveGov](https://www.executivegov.com/articles/army-garciga-application-owners-ai-records-mgmt)). The research found no guidance tying AI transcripts to the Privacy Act. Under that Act's general rule, though, a tool that files transcripts by speaker identity, through voiceprints or per-person histories, could create a "system of records" that needs a SORN and a privacy impact assessment ([CRS R46238](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46238)). This follows from the general rules and is not stated in any notetaker-specific guidance.

States are blunter. Georgia, Missouri, Arizona and Wisconsin all treat AI recordings or transcripts as public records subject to retention. Missouri's SP-19 covers even unused AI drafts under its Sunshine Law ([MO SP-19](https://oa.mo.gov/sites/default/files/SP-19%20Acceptable%20Use%20for%20Artificial%20Intelligence.pdf); [AZ 2000PR](https://aset.az.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/2000PR%20-%20Use%20of%20Generative%20AI%20Procedure.pdf); [WI DET AUP](https://det.wi.gov/Documents/AUP%20-%20Effective%20Date%203.10.2025.pdf)). A UNC School of Government analysis says content and purpose, not storage location, decide record status under North Carolina law ([UNC SOG](https://canons.sog.unc.edu/blog/2026/03/11/the-intersection-of-artificial-intelligence-and-the-public-records-act/)). For Meeting Copilot this cuts against the Live-only mode in government. Whether a never-stored transcript is a "record" at all is an open question. But once a summary is shared or relied on as minutes, the agency may be *required* to keep it. In a government deployment, "save nothing" has to be a policy the agency controls and documents, alongside export into the agency's recordkeeping system and legal-hold support, not a default the user picks alone.

## State law makes consent and voiceprints the real liability

Federal wiretap law is one-party consent, but roughly 10 to 13 states require every party's consent, depending on how hybrid states are counted. The core list is California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania and Washington, with Connecticut, Michigan, Nevada and Oregon treated as hybrid ([RecordingLaw](https://recordinglaw.com/united-states-recording-laws/); [Kilpatrick Townsend](https://ktslaw.com/Insights/Alert/2024/7/Wiretap-Laws-in-the-United-States)). Only the California, Illinois and Washington citations were confirmed in the research; the others are **unverified**. The critical point for an on-device design is that most of these statutes forbid *intercepting or acquiring* content, not only keeping a recording. A law professor quoted by CNBC in October 2026 says New Hampshire's "aural or other acquisition" language could reach on-device audio features that save nothing. A Massachusetts defense firm says that state's statute "may" be violated without a saved audio file, and notes that no appellate court has decided the question ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/18/ai-wearables-privacy-wiretap-laws.html); [Serpa Law](https://www.serpalaw.com/boston-criminal-law-updates/ai-wearables-massachusetts-wiretap-law/)). The same Massachusetts source cites a 1976 case holding that a recording everyone knows about is not an interception. That is why notice to *all participants*, not only an indicator on the user's own screen, is the central safeguard.

The litigation explains why governments now distrust bots. In *In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation* (N.D. Cal. No. 5:25-cv-06911), an order reported as dated **13 August 2026** let ECPA, CIPA § 631 and BIPA voiceprint claims proceed. Commentators say the court found Otter plausibly a "third-party eavesdropper" because it *keeps recordings and trains its own models on them* ([Crandall Consulting](https://crandall.consulting/website-tracking-cases/in-re-otter-ai-privacy-litigation/); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/invited-participant-or-third-party-eavesdropper-court-holds-otterai-third-party)). The order itself was not read, and several accounts come from vendor blogs, so the date and reasoning need checking against the docket. Fireflies faces BIPA class actions over its speaker-recognition feature, including *Cruz* (venue reported inconsistently) and *Fricker*, No. 1:26-cv-02675 (N.D. Ill.) ([Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/illinois-bipa-suit-targets-ai-note-takers-practical-lessons-for-meeting-transcription); [Top Class Actions](https://topclassactions.com/lawsuit-settlements/lawsuit-news/fireflies-ai-sued-over-alleged-unlawful-data-collection-from-meeting-participants)). On this theory a local-only tool that neither keeps nor trains on data looks like an extension of its user rather than an eavesdropper, which is a real advantage. It does not, however, cure the all-party-consent problem.

**Voice enrollment is the highest-risk feature in the product.** BIPA names voiceprints and allows $1,000 or $5,000 in liquidated damages. Since a 2024 amendment, damages count per person rather than per scan, and the Seventh Circuit held in April 2026 that the change applies retroactively. Consent can now be given by electronic signature ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/08/illinois-bipa-biometrics-law-amended-for-damages); [ABA Business Law Today](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-may/7th-circuit-holds-bipa-damages-remedy-applies-retroactively/)). BIPA can apply whenever *any* participant is physically in Illinois ([Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/illinois-bipa-suit-targets-ai-note-takers-practical-lessons-for-meeting-transcription)). Texas CUBI also covers voiceprints and is enforced only by the attorney general, at up to $25,000 per violation ([Texas AG](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/enforcing-privacy-and-data-security-laws-protect-texans/biometric-identifier-act)). Enrolling only the user's own voice, with a written release and a published retention-and-destruction policy, is defensible. Matching other participants' voices across meetings is what the Fireflies suits target. Whether anonymous "Speaker 1/Speaker 2" diarization embeddings count as voiceprints is unsettled.

State CIO policies are where notetakers are actually named. **Georgia** prohibits third-party AI note-taking tools in state Teams meetings and on any other conferencing platform. It permits Teams' built-in transcription and requires prior Georgia Technology Authority (GTA) authorization for any regularly used transcription or note-taking AI ([Georgia AI guidance](https://ai.georgia.gov/guidance/guidance-state-organizations); [GTA AI Tool Use](https://gta.georgia.gov/policies-and-programs/artificial-intelligence/ai-tool-use)). Neither page could be fetched in full, so the exact wording and date are unconfirmed. **Missouri** bars AI meeting bots from sensitive meetings unless they are an approved enterprise service. **Maryland** approves only Gemini for Google Meet and Copilot for Teams and reviews anything else ([Maryland DoIT](https://doit.maryland.gov/policies/ai/Pages/AI-governance-card-call-recording-transcription-tools.aspx)). **Indiana's** records agency advises against admitting AI notetakers to remote government meetings ([B Square Bulletin](https://bsquarebulletin.com/live-streamed-govt-meetings-now-required-by-indiana-law-state-records-agency-advises-banning-ai-notetakers/)). **Bellevue, WA** disables most notetakers in non-public Teams meetings unless an exception is approved, but tells staff they may not stop the public from recording public meetings ([Bellevue](https://bellevuewa.gov/ai-notetakers-guidance)). Broader frameworks add duties at the edges. Texas TRAIGA, effective 1 January 2026, requires agencies to disclose when people are interacting with AI ([Texas House analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/analysis/html/HB00149H.htm)). California's EO N-5-26 (March 2026) orders certification standards for AI vendors seeking state contracts ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/04/california-governor-issues-executive-order-on-ai-procurement-standards)). Colorado narrowed its AI Act in May 2026, with the main duties starting 1 January 2027 ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/06/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-ai-act)). Georgia's split is instructive: it bans third-party *bots* but allows the platform's own transcription. That suggests the objection is to outside parties and outside custody, not to transcription as such.

## Contractors answer to the host first and the CUI rules second

No binding federal rule names AI notetakers for contractors. Their obligations come in three layers. The first is the hosting agency's rules of behavior and its default that unapproved AI is prohibited. The second is the contract's CUI clauses. The third is the contractor's own acceptable-use policy. Consultant guidance for defense contractors consistently says not to put CUI into personal AI tools, including consumer transcription, and to name approved tools explicitly ([GS Consulting](https://gsconsultingllc.com/insights/dod-contractors-ai-cui-risk); [VSO Inc.](https://vso-inc.com/how-to-use-ai-without-breaking-cmmc-compliance-a-defense-contractors-guide/)). The clearest published host rule is the Education Department's. It allows AI recording or transcription of grant meetings only with prior written *and* verbal notice to all attendees, and ED staff may require the tool to be turned off ([ED](https://www.ed.gov/grants-and-programs/apply-grant/getting-started-discretionary-grant-applications/grants-and-artificial-intelligence-ai)). The 2025 reports of Gemini recording USAID meetings without consent show what hosts are reacting to ([IT Brew](https://itbrew.com/stories/2025/02/13/google-s-gemini-ai-recorded-meetings-without-consent-two-usaid-staffers-tell-it-brew)). For the user's own situation this settles the first question. Where the client disallows AI notetakers, a bot-free tool running quietly on the contractor's laptop is still an AI notetaker under any reasonable reading of that rule. Using it without the host's written permission is a contract-relationship risk whatever the architecture.

The cyber rules govern where the data may go once permission exists. Meeting audio or a transcript that contains CUI is itself CUI. Under 32 CFR Part 2002 and **DFARS 252.204-7012**, it must sit on systems that meet **NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2**, the version DoD still requires through a class deviation despite Rev 3's release in 2024 ([eCFR 32 CFR 2002](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-B/chapter-XX/part-2002); [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/05/us-dod-issues-class-deviation-delaying-dfars-implementation-of-upcoming-nist-sp-800-171-revision-3)). Any external cloud that stores or processes it must meet FedRAMP Moderate-equivalent requirements ([DFARS 252.204-7012](https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/252.204-7012-safeguarding-covered-defense-information-and-cyber-incident-reporting.)). **CMMC Phase 1** self-assessments began 10 November 2025. **Phase 2** (third-party Level 2 assessments), due 10 November 2026, was **suspended on 13 July 2026** pending a reform review, and no restart had been confirmed by late November ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260720-pentagon-suspends-cmmc-phase-2-requirements-and-launches-review-of-cybersecurity-certification-program); [Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/defense-news/2026/10/rfi-offers-path-forward-for-dod-with-cmmc)). The 7012 and 800-171 duties, and False Claims Act exposure, continue during the pause ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/07/department-of-war-suspends-cmmc-phase-ii-requirements-but-cybersecurity-obligations-remain)). This is where local-only pays off. A cloud copilot pulls the vendor *and every speech-recognition or LLM sub-processor* into the FedRAMP-equivalent test. A local-only app only has to be documented as an asset in the contractor's existing system security plan, on a company laptop that is already inside the 800-171 boundary. On a personal laptop it would not be compliant at all.

## What approval actually requires for each Meeting Copilot mode

FedRAMP covers cloud services that "create, collect, process, store, or maintain Federal information on behalf of a Federal agency." It excludes software distributed for installation inside a customer's own environment, and **the agency alone decides scope** ([FedRAMP M-24-15 scope](https://www.fedramp.gov/docs/authority/m-24-15/scope/)). The research found no FedRAMP text that explicitly addresses installed desktop or mobile software, so the conclusion that a zero-egress app is out of scope is an inference from the cloud-service definition, though a strong one. For the Cloud mode, FedRAMP itself changed in 2026. The **2026 Consolidated Rules (25 June 2026)** made 20x generally available and replaced impact levels with Classes A to D, with Class C roughly equal to the old Moderate. Class B and C 20x pipelines opened on 31 August 2026, and new Rev5 submissions close after **11 June 2027** ([FedRAMP CR26 announcement](https://www.fedramp.gov/2026-06-25-propelling-change-fedramp-launches-consolidated-rules-for-2026/); [FedRAMP help](https://help.fedramp.gov/hc/en-us/articles/53961794314779-What-milestone-must-be-reached-by-11-June-2027-for-a-new-authorization-effort-to-proceed-under-Rev-5)). Legacy authorizations cost "hundreds of thousands of dollars" on the government side alone, and vendor estimates put assessor fees at $100k to $500k with 12 to 18 months for Rev5 ([FedRAMP RFC-0023](https://www.fedramp.gov/rfcs/0023); [Secureframe, vendor](https://secureframe.com/hub/fedramp/costs.md)).

| Mode | What governs approval | Main blockers | Verdict for a government client |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloud | FedRAMP Class C (20x), agency ATO, M-25-22 data/IP terms; every "meeting" provider also authorized | Cost and time; sub-processors; Otter-style third-party theory if any provider retains or trains | Long-term path; not viable for this client soon |
| Local-only | Agency approved-software list or ATO-by-inheritance on the managed endpoint (NIST SP 800-37/800-53); contractor SSP under 800-171 Rev 2 | Host permission; proving zero egress; records export | Best near-term path |
| Live-only | Same as Local-only, plus agency records policy | Conflicts with retention once output is shared or used as minutes; consent laws unaffected | Offer as an agency-controlled policy, not a user toggle |
| Voice enrollment | BIPA/CUBI written consent and retention schedule; possible Privacy Act SORN if retrieved by identity | Per-person damages; non-consenting participants | Off by default; enroll only the user's own voice |

For a local-only app, "approval" means the agency's technical reference model or software allow-list review, usually led by the CISO or ISSO. The app is assessed as part of the managed endpoint's existing authorization under NIST SP 800-37 and inherits most SP 800-53 controls from the laptop ([NIST SP 800-37](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/37/r2/final)). On top of that comes an AI use-case review under the agency's own policy, such as DOT's July 2026 policy covering AI on DOT systems ([DOT AI policy](https://www.transportation.gov/sites/dot.gov/files/ai_policy_2026-07-10-508.docx)). **OMB M-26-05 (23 January 2026)** dropped the mandatory secure-software attestation form, so SBOM and attestation requests now vary by agency ([OMB M-26-05](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/M-26-05-Adopting-a-Risk-based-Approach-to-Software-and-Hardware-Security.pdf)). Every federal purchase also needs a **Section 508** Accessibility Conformance Report on the VPAT 2.5 "508" edition, against WCAG 2.0 AA for software ([Section508.gov](https://www.section508.gov/sell/acr-vpat-faq/)). For DoD, the current reference for a stronger credential is NIAP's Protection Profile for Application Software v2.0 (May 2025) ([NIAP PP](https://commoncriteria.github.io/pp/application/application.html)). The research did not confirm when NIAP evaluation is mandatory for desktop apps. Put together, the vendor package starts with a data-flow and network statement, backed by packet captures, showing that updates, licensing and model downloads carry no audio or text, plus a verifiable offline mode. It adds a one-page FedRAMP scope memo measured against M-24-15, control mappings to SP 800-53 and 800-171 Rev 2, and FIPS 140-3 validated encryption at rest, normally through the operating system's own modules. Supply-chain and AI evidence come next: an SBOM, and model cards confirming on-device inference and no training on customer data. It closes with an independently tested 508 conformance report and product features for all-party notice and host consent, a visible indicator, retention controls and records export.

The research found **no public example of a federal agency formally approving a specific on-device AI notetaker**. Offline speech products such as Speechmatics on-device and Moonshine through Carahsoft are marketed to government without any authorization claims ([Speechmatics](https://www.speechmatics.com/speech-to-text/on-device); [Carahsoft](https://www.carahsoft.com/moonshine/solutions)). Meeting Copilot would be early, not following a proven path. One practical gap also stands out. The repository's current architecture, a FastAPI backend routing through LiteLLM to cloud providers with Supabase sync, is a Cloud-mode design. A credible Local-only mode needs on-device speech recognition and an on-device LLM, with the backend off by default and phase 3 sync packaged separately so it cannot quietly bring the product back into FedRAMP scope.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question the user should ask the client. Bot-free and local-only remove the two objections that drive most government bans: an outside party in the meeting, and an outside party holding the data. They do not touch the remaining two, consent and records. So the winning pitch to a client that disallows AI notetakers is not "this isn't really a notetaker." It is a request for a scoped exception, in the form Georgia, Missouri and Bellevue already use: a written approval for named meetings, with all-party notice of the kind ED requires, enrollment of the user's voice only, outputs exported into the client's records system, and a zero-egress statement the client's ISSO can test. The contractor's own security officer has to sign off as well, because the laptop holds CUI-bearing transcripts inside the 800-171 boundary.

Two dates set the timing. CMMC Phase 2 is paused but expected back in narrower form, and new FedRAMP Rev5 starts close on 11 June 2027. Both reward an architecture whose default compliance surface is the user's own managed device. The likely competitor in most of these meetings is Microsoft's own Teams recap rather than Otter, and its uneven availability in GCC High and DoD will not last. Local-only is therefore best positioned as the option for meetings that agencies will not send to any cloud: personnel, legal, ITAR and in-person sessions.
